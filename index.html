How it actually happens

One email.
One very bad month.

Most small-business breaches aren’t movie-style hacking. They’re a convincing email, a reused password, and nobody double-checking. Here’s how a common scam plays out.

Example scenario: a composite of a common pattern called business email compromise. Names and times are illustrative.

  1. MON · 8:14 AM

    An email from your supplier

    Same name, same logo, same signature. “We changed banks — please send this month’s payment to our new account.”

  2. MON · 8:31 AM

    It looks routine, so it gets paid

    Your bookkeeper updates the details. Nothing seems off — the attacker has been reading the real email thread for weeks after guessing a reused password.

  3. 3 WEEKS LATER

    The real supplier calls

    “Where’s our payment?” The money went to a criminal’s account and is long gone. And the attacker still has access to your inbox.

What would have stopped it

  • Two-step login on email. The stolen password alone wouldn’t have opened the inbox.
  • A call-to-confirm rule. Any bank change gets verified by phone, using a number you already have.
  • Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC). Makes look-alike emails far easier to catch.
